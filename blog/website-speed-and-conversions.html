Site Speed Is a Conversion Problem, Not a Tech One

Digital Picto November 7, 2026 8 min read

Site speed affects revenue twice: visitors leave before the page renders, and both Google and Meta factor page experience into how often your ads serve. That makes speed a commercial problem rather than an engineering preference, and it is usually fixable without a rebuild.

What actually makes sites slow?

Uncompressed images, render-blocking scripts, and third-party tags nobody audits.

Images are the most common single cause and the easiest to fix: correct formats, correct dimensions, and lazy loading below the fold.

Third-party scripts accumulate silently. Most sites carry tags for tools they stopped using.

The mechanism is abandonment, not patience. A visitor who arrived from an ad has no investment in your page; the back button costs them nothing, and the next option is one tap away. Every second of blank screen is a chance to take it.

This hits paid traffic hardest, because you paid for the click that the slow page then discards. Organic visitors who searched for you specifically are more forgiving; someone who tapped an ad in a feed is not.

How does speed affect paid campaigns?

Page experience is an input to quality scoring, so a slow page costs you impressions as well as visitors.

This is why we build the landing page before launching campaigns rather than after. In one regulated-category campaign, page optimisation came first specifically to raise quality score and win more impressions.

A slow page raises costs on both sides: fewer conversions from the traffic, and less traffic for the same budget.

Largest Contentful Paint is usually an image or a headline, and usually fixable: compress and correctly size the hero image, serve it in a modern format, and stop lazy-loading the thing that is already on screen.

Interaction to Next Paint is usually JavaScript blocking the main thread, and the honest fix is removing scripts rather than optimising them. Cumulative Layout Shift is almost always missing width and height attributes on images, or a font swap, and both are cheap to correct.

What should you fix first?

Whatever is delaying the largest element the visitor is waiting for.

Measure before optimising. Core Web Vitals field data tells you what real visitors experience, which often differs from a lab test on a fast connection.

Prioritise by impact rather than by how satisfying the fix feels.

Measure in the field as well as the lab. A synthetic test from a fast connection will pass a page that fails on a phone on mobile data, which is the condition most of your visitors are actually in.

Test the page people land on, not the homepage. Landing pages accumulate tracking scripts, chat widgets and embeds that the homepage never carries, and they are usually the slowest pages on the site.

Does this mean a rebuild?

Rarely. Most meaningful gains come from images, scripts and caching on the existing site.

Rebuilds are sometimes justified, but they are rarely the cheapest route to a faster page.

Our web design service starts with measurement so the work is aimed at what is actually slow.

Third-party scripts are the recurring cause because each one is added by someone solving a real problem, and nobody owns removing them. Audit the list quarterly and ask what breaks if each is deleted.

The two that most often earn their cost are analytics and the conversion tag. Most of the rest are a chat widget nobody answers and a pixel for a channel that was switched off a year ago.

How fast is fast enough?

Fast enough that the visitor never notices loading, which in practice means the main content visible in about two seconds on a mid-range phone.

Chasing a perfect score past that point has diminishing returns compared with fixing the headline or the form. Speed is a floor, not a lever.

The exception is when the page is genuinely slow. Going from eight seconds to two is not an optimisation, it is the difference between a page that converts and one that does not.

Where does speed fit against other conversion work?

First, because every other improvement is measured through it.

A/B testing a headline on a page that loses a third of its visitors before render produces results you cannot trust. Fix the floor, then test.

We treat it as part of the build rather than a later optimisation, which is also why our landing page checklist starts with load behaviour before it reaches copy.
