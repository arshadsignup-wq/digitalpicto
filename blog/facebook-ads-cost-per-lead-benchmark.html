What Should a Facebook Lead Actually Cost?

Digital Picto October 15, 2026 9 min read

There is no single correct Facebook cost per lead. Across accounts we have run, cost per lead has landed anywhere between $3.91 and $66.60, and both were good results in their category. A $5 lead in B2B services and a $66 lead in roofing can be equally profitable, because what matters is not the cost of the lead but what the lead is worth when it closes.

What is a good cost per lead on Facebook Ads?

A good cost per lead is one your contract value can absorb profitably. That is the whole answer, and it is why published cross-industry averages are close to useless as a target.

Our published campaigns span a wide range. A US hair and scalp clinic settled at $3.91 per lead after 35 campaigns. A B2B company selling packages above $1,000 reached $5.08 per lead. A roofing company came in at $66.60 per lead.

The roofing figure is seventeen times the clinic figure, and both were strong outcomes. Roofing is one of the most expensive verticals on Meta, with published 2026 benchmarks above $115, so $66.60 sits roughly 40% under category. A roofing job worth several thousand dollars absorbs a $66 lead in a way a low-ticket product never could.

Benchmarks are only comparable within a category, a geography and an offer. A $5 lead for a roof inspection and a $5 lead for a commercial software demo describe entirely different businesses, and averaging them produces a number that describes neither.

Why do cross-industry cost per lead averages mislead?

A single average blends categories whose economics have nothing in common, so it cannot tell you whether your number is good or bad.

The variables that actually move cost per lead are contract value, competition in the auction, how narrow the buying intent is, and the offer itself. None of those are captured by an average.

We saw this inside a single account. Running two campaigns for a US lending company with the same method and the same operator produced $7.98 for lending and $11.21 for lease buyout. Lease buyout is a narrower intent, so the addressable audience is smaller and the auction thinner. The $3.23 gap is a property of the offer, not of the execution.

Work backwards from the deal instead. Deal value, close rate from lead to customer, and the margin you are willing to spend give you a ceiling, and the ceiling is the only benchmark that can tell you whether to keep spending.

That calculation also makes the right decision obvious when a channel gets more expensive. A rising cost per lead is only a problem relative to the ceiling, not relative to last month.

How do you reduce cost per lead on Facebook?

Reductions usually come in distinct steps from separate causes, not as one gradual slide. Fix measurement first, then audience, then creative.

In the hair clinic account the drop happened in two clear stages: $86 to roughly $14, then $14 to $3.91. The first step came from fixing attribution and narrowing the audience. The second came almost entirely from creative.

That split matters. The account had two separate problems, and solving only one of them would have stalled it at $14. If your cost per lead has plateaued after an audience change, creative is usually the remaining variable.

Structural faults inflate cost per lead more than creative does: overlapping audiences bidding against each other, too few creatives for delivery to optimise between, and broken or duplicated events.

On the Scalp Shop account the cost per lead fell from $86 to $14 and then to $3.91 after that structural work, finishing at a 94/100 Meta Opportunity Score.

Does a full-funnel structure lower cost per lead?

Yes, when the warm audience it builds is actually retargeted. Awareness spend that is never retargeted is just awareness spend.

For a B2B client we ran a $100 awareness campaign first, reaching 43,927 people, then ran the lead campaign only against that warm pool. The result was 64 leads at $5.08, which came in 63% below Meta's own $13.19 benchmark for comparable ad sets.

Running lead ads cold to the same audience would almost certainly have cost more. The $100 was not buying awareness as a goal in itself; it was buying cheaper leads later.

Treat lead quality as part of the cost. A channel producing leads at half the price that close at a third of the rate is the more expensive channel, and only pipeline data shows it.

That requires reporting the close rate back by source, which is the reconciliation most accounts never build and the one that makes every other number trustworthy.

What cost per lead should you budget for?

Work backwards from close rate and contract value rather than forwards from a benchmark.

If you close one lead in ten and a customer is worth $2,000, each lead carries $200 of value and a $60 lead is comfortably profitable. If you close one in ten and a customer is worth $150, the same $60 lead loses money.

Set the ceiling from that calculation, then judge the campaign against your own ceiling. Our paid advertising service starts every engagement with this number, because without it there is no way to say whether a campaign is working.

What if your cost per lead is rising?

Check audience saturation and creative supply before concluding the channel is exhausted.

Rising frequency on a stable audience raises cost mechanically. Fresh creative or a wider audience usually resets it, and neither is evidence the channel has stopped working.

Seasonal auction pressure is the other common cause. Competitors entering the same auction raise everyone's costs, and the response is a ceiling calculation rather than a panic.
