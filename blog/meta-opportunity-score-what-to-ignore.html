Meta's Opportunity Score: What to Act On

By Digital Picto November 2, 2026 8 min read

Meta's Opportunity Score surfaces account recommendations, some structural and genuinely useful, others simply nudging you toward broader targeting and higher budgets. Act on the ones about tracking, structure and creative volume. Treat budget and audience-expansion prompts as suggestions to evaluate, not instructions.

Which recommendations are worth acting on?

Anything about event tracking, duplicate audiences, fragmented budgets, or too few creatives in an ad set.

These flag real structural faults that limit delivery regardless of what Meta gains from fixing them.

On the Scalp Shop account structural work across 35 campaigns took cost per lead from $86 to $3.91, finishing at a 94/100 Meta Opportunity Score.

Audience overlap is the one most worth chasing, because it is invisible otherwise. Two ad sets bidding on substantially the same people drive each other's costs up, and the symptom looks like a competitive auction rather than a self-inflicted one.

Creative volume is the second. An ad set with one creative gives delivery nothing to optimise between, and fatigue then reads as a targeting problem.

Which should you evaluate rather than accept?

Budget increases and audience broadening, because both increase Meta's revenue whether or not they improve yours.

Broad targeting genuinely works in some accounts. It is still a decision that belongs to whoever owns the margin.

The test is whether the recommendation improves your cost per outcome, which only your own data can answer.

The test is whether the recommendation changes your cost per outcome or Meta's revenue per account. Broad targeting often does both, which is why it deserves a real test rather than either reflexive acceptance or reflexive suspicion.

Run it as a test with a defined budget and window, against your current structure, and let your own numbers decide. 'Meta recommended it' is not a result.

Does the score itself matter?

Only as a proxy. A higher score correlates with fewer structural faults, not with better results by itself.

We treat it as a checklist that someone else maintains, useful for catching things we missed.

Chasing the number for its own sake is how accounts end up consolidated into configurations nobody chose.

Score movement is most useful as a before-and-after on structural work. The Scalp Shop account finished at 94/100 because audiences, event setup and creative testing were fixed, and the cost per lead fell alongside it.

The score did not cause that. It described an account that had stopped getting in its own way.

What does a clean account structure look like?

One clear objective per campaign, non-overlapping audiences, enough creative to let delivery optimise, and verified events.

That is the structure our paid advertising service builds toward, and most Opportunity Score warnings disappear as a side effect.

Fixing the cause removes the warning. Dismissing the warning does not fix the cause.

Verified events deserve their own mention because they underpin everything else. Server-side and browser events that disagree, or a purchase event firing on a page view, will corrupt optimisation no matter how clean the campaign structure looks.

What does Meta not tell you?

Anything about your offer, your page, or your follow-up, which is where most underperformance actually lives.

The Opportunity Score can only see the account. A campaign with perfect structure pointing at a page that loads in eight seconds will score well and perform badly, and nothing in the interface will say so.

This is the limit worth remembering. Platform diagnostics optimise the platform's half of the funnel.

How should you work through the list?

Tracking first, then overlap, then creative, then budget.

That order puts the changes that improve measurement before the changes measured by it, which saves re-running decisions on bad data.

Dismiss what you have deliberately decided against, and write down why. Otherwise the same recommendation returns every month and gets re-litigated by whoever opens the account next.
