Negative Keywords: Where Ad Budget Quietly Leaks

By Digital Picto November 1, 2026 8 min read

Negative keywords tell Google which searches should never trigger your ads. Without them, broad and phrase match will spend your budget on people researching, looking for jobs, or seeking free alternatives. In both Google Ads accounts we rebuilt, negative keyword filtering was part of the fix that moved cost per click down and ROI up.

How does budget leak without them?

Broad match interprets intent generously, which means it will find searches you never intended to pay for.

Job seekers, students, people looking for DIY instructions and competitors researching you all click. None of them buy.

At scale this is not a rounding error. It is often the difference between an account that works and one that does not.

Broad and phrase match are matched on meaning now, not just on words, which means the queries you serve on can drift a long way from the keyword you bought. Negatives are the only control you have over that drift.

The cost compounds quietly. Irrelevant clicks do not just waste money, they teach smart bidding about the wrong audience, so a month of unfiltered traffic degrades the optimisation as well as the budget.

Where do you find the terms to exclude?

The search terms report, which shows what people actually typed rather than what you targeted.

Read it weekly at first. Every irrelevant term you find is budget you will stop spending from that point forward.

Patterns matter more than individual terms: 'free', 'jobs', 'salary', 'DIY' and 'how to' are usually worth excluding as phrases.

Read the search terms report weekly at first, then monthly once it stabilises. Sort by cost rather than by impressions; the expensive irrelevant query matters more than the frequent one.

Look for three patterns: the wrong intent, free, DIY, jobs, salary, course; the wrong product, a competitor's model name or a service you do not offer; and the wrong geography, a city outside your service area appearing inside a broad term.

What did it change in practice?

In two account rebuilds, negative keyword work sat alongside restructure and tracking fixes.

For a sauna retailer, the combination moved ROI up 87% and cost per click down 25% across 2,416 conversions.

For a legal services account, the same approach contributed to ROI up 31% and cost per click down 17%.

Put structural negatives at account level through a shared list, so a decision made once applies everywhere and new campaigns inherit it. Keep campaign and ad group negatives for the ones that are only wrong in that context.

The distinction matters because the same word can be a negative in one campaign and a core keyword in another. 'Free' is poison in a lead-gen campaign and essential in one advertising a free audit.

Can you exclude too much?

Yes. Over-filtering starves the campaign of volume and can stop delivery entirely.

Exclude terms that are clearly wrong, not terms that are merely unproven. A query with no conversions yet is data you have not finished collecting.

Review the list periodically. Terms excluded during one campaign may be relevant to the next.

Match type applies to negatives too, and getting it wrong is how advertisers accidentally block their own traffic. A broad negative blocks any query containing those words in any order, which is rarely what anyone intends.

Use phrase negatives for most exclusions and exact negatives for single queries you want to stop. Reserve broad negatives for words that could never be relevant in any combination.

Can you have too many negatives?

Yes. An over-pruned account starves itself of the discovery that finds new winning queries.

Every negative narrows the space the campaign can explore. A list grown reactively over years will eventually exclude variations that would have converted, and nobody will ever see them to know.

Review the list periodically and remove exclusions that were added for a product or geography you have since changed. Negatives accumulate; nothing removes them automatically.

How do negatives relate to ad group structure?

They are the other half of the same job.

In a single themed ad group structure, cross-group negatives keep each group serving only its own intent, which is what lets the ad copy stay specific.

Without them, two groups compete for the same query and the looser one usually wins, which is how a carefully built structure quietly collapses back into a broad campaign.
