Facebook Ads for Home Service Businesses

By Digital Picto November 9, 2026 9 min read

Home service advertising on Meta works when three things line up: local targeting, visible proof of past work, and immediate follow-up. On a roofing campaign we produced 70 leads at $66.60 each, roughly 40% under the published category benchmark of $115 or more. The follow-up speed mattered as much as the targeting.

Why does speed to lead dominate this category?

Because homeowners contact several providers and the first useful reply usually wins the job.

A lead that waits an hour is competing against whoever answered in two minutes.

That is why we pair ads with missed-call text-back and instant notification rather than treating the form submission as the finish line.

A homeowner with a leaking roof is not browsing. They are working through a list, and the list shortens with every call that gets answered. The provider who books the inspection first usually never faces a comparison, because the comparison stops being worth the homeowner's afternoon.

This changes what the ad has to do. It does not need to close anyone. It needs to produce a contactable lead fast enough that your follow-up arrives while the problem is still urgent.

What creative actually performs?

Real work. Before and after photos of jobs you have done, shot on site.

Polished stock imagery underperforms a clear photo of a finished roof in a recognisable neighbourhood.

On the roofing campaign that approach produced 70 leads at $66.60 each from $4,661.96 of spend.

Shoot on the job. A phone photo of a finished roof with the house, the gutters and the street visible reads as real; a stock aerial of a suburb reads as an ad. The same holds for driveways, fences, HVAC installs and bathroom remodels.

Caption the location. Naming the suburb or county does two things at once: it tells delivery who to show it to, and it tells the homeowner this company works where they live.

How should targeting be set up?

Service area first, then let delivery optimise inside it.

Over-layering interests on top of a tight geography shrinks the audience below the size delivery needs to learn.

Geography is the one constraint that is genuinely non-negotiable for a business that sends vans to houses.

Layering interests on top of a tight radius is the most common self-inflicted wound in this category. A 15-mile service area already restricts the audience severely; adding a homeowner interest filter on top can shrink it below the volume delivery needs to find the people who actually convert.

What happens after the form is submitted?

A booked appointment, or nothing. The gap between those two is operational, not creative.

Reminders, follow-up sequences and a pipeline that shows which leads were never called are the difference.

We build that layer in GoHighLevel so the ads and the follow-up share one system.

Build the list of leads nobody called. That single report changes behaviour faster than any coaching conversation, because it makes the leak visible and attributable. Most home service businesses discover their worst channel is not a channel at all, it is Thursday afternoon.

How do you handle seasonality?

Keep the campaign running at reduced budget through the quiet months rather than switching it off.

Restarting a paused campaign restarts the learning phase, so you pay for re-learning exactly when demand is returning and competition is climbing. A low continuous budget is usually cheaper than a seasonal relaunch.

Shift the offer instead of the switch. Inspections, maintenance plans and pre-season checks keep the pipeline warm in a month when nobody is searching for emergency repair, and they fill the calendar that the busy season then competes for.

The accounts that struggle most are the ones that treat advertising as a tap. Demand is seasonal; the algorithm's memory is not.

What should you measure?

Cost per booked job, not cost per lead.

Cost per lead is the number the ad platform can see, which is why it becomes the number everyone reports. It is also the number that hides a broken phone process.

On the roofing campaign, $66.60 per lead was only defensible because the leads were being worked. The same cost per lead with a two-day callback would have been an expensive way to generate nothing.
