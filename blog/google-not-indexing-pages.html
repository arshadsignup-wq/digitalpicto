Google Crawled But Did Not Index: How to Fix It

By Digital Picto October 24, 2026 9 min read

If Search Console reports pages as crawled but not indexed, Google has seen them and decided they are not worth storing. The usual causes are thin or duplicated content, canonical conflicts, redirect chains, or robots directives. Indexation has to be fixed before new content is published, because content added to a site Google is already refusing to index competes with that problem first.

What is the difference between crawled and discovered?

'Crawled, currently not indexed' means Google fetched the page and declined it. 'Discovered, currently not indexed' means it has not fetched it yet.

The two point to different problems. Crawled-not-indexed is usually a quality or duplication judgement. Discovered-not-indexed is more often a crawl budget or site structure issue.

Both are worth treating as signals about the site rather than noise. At any meaningful scale, they tell you Google has formed a view.

Work in order: is the page crawlable, is it indexable, and is it worth indexing. Most diagnosis fails because it starts at the third question while the answer sits in the first.

The URL Inspection tool answers the first two directly, and it answers them for the specific URL rather than for the site. Site-level assumptions are where this investigation usually goes wrong.

What causes Google to refuse a page?

Thin content, near-duplication across templated pages, conflicting canonical tags, redirect chains, or an accidental noindex.

Templated pages are the most common culprit on e-commerce and location-based sites: hundreds of pages differing only by a product name or suburb read as duplicates.

We saw this directly on a grocery delivery site where a large share of the catalogue was being held out of the index. The fix was writing the content Google was skipping rather than adding more pages.

The accidental blocks are more common than the deliberate ones: a noindex tag left from staging, a robots.txt rule written for a subdirectory that matches more than intended, or a canonical tag pointing at a different page.

Canonical mistakes are the quietest. A page that canonicalises to the homepage is being explicitly told not to exist as itself, and nothing in the interface flags it as an error.

Why fix indexation before writing content?

Because nothing compounds until it is fixed. Publishing onto an unindexed site produces more unindexed pages.

This is the sequencing mistake we see most often. A site invests in a content programme while the underlying indexation problem goes untouched, and six months later the traffic line is flat.

Search Console is the starting document for this work, not the keyword tool.

Discovered but not indexed usually means a quality or priority judgement, not a technical fault. The page was found, assessed, and deprioritised, which is a content problem wearing a technical costume.

Thin pages, near-duplicates and pages reachable only from a sitemap are the three recurring causes. Internal links from relevant pages are the most reliable remedy.

How do you actually fix it?

Clear the technical blockers first, then give the remaining pages a reason to exist.

Resolve 404s, redirect chains, canonical conflicts and robots.txt flags. Then address the content itself: unique descriptions, genuine FAQs, and enough substance that the page answers something.

On the catering site we rebuilt, the audit found 4 errors, 402 warnings and 66 notices before any content work began. Clearing that was the precondition for the traffic growth that followed.

Submitting a URL for indexing is a request, not an instruction, and submitting it repeatedly does not strengthen it. If a page is not indexed after a genuine fix, the fix was not the problem.

Give a real change a week or two before concluding. Re-crawling is not instant, and the common error is stacking three more changes on top before the first was evaluated.

How long does reindexing take?

Days to weeks, depending on crawl frequency and site authority. Request indexing for priority pages rather than waiting.

Submit the important URLs through Search Console directly and make sure they are in the sitemap. Internal links from pages Google already indexes also help it find them again.

Our technical SEO work treats indexation as the first milestone of any engagement, because every other metric depends on it.

Do sitemaps help?

They help discovery, not qualification.

A sitemap tells search engines a URL exists. It does not argue that the URL matters, which is what internal links do.

A page that appears only in the sitemap and nowhere in the site's own navigation or content is being described as unimportant by the site itself.

When is not being indexed the right outcome?

More often than people assume.

Tag archives, filtered variants, internal search results and thin paginated pages consume crawl attention and compete with the pages you want ranking. Excluding them deliberately is good housekeeping.

Decide which pages matter, make those excellent and well-linked, and let the rest go. A smaller indexed footprint of strong pages outperforms a large one of weak ones.
