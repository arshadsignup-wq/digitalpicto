Klaviyo or Mailchimp: Which Fits Your Business?

Digital Picto November 12, 2026 8 min read

Klaviyo suits ecommerce, where segmentation depends on purchase and browse behaviour. Mailchimp suits businesses sending broadcasts and simple automations without deep product data. If your segments are defined by what people bought, Klaviyo earns its price; if they are defined by who signed up, it usually does not.

What does Klaviyo do that Mailchimp does not?

Segment on product and purchase behaviour natively, which is what ecommerce flows depend on.

Abandoned browse, post-purchase sequences keyed to the specific item, and replenishment timing all need that data model.

We have built in both platforms across the 28 industries in our email portfolio, and the platform choice follows the data, not the brand.

The distinction is the data model, not the feature list. Klaviyo is built to treat a customer as a stream of events, so a segment can be a sentence like 'bought twice, not in ninety days, browsed the category last week'. That sentence is the product.

Mailchimp treats a contact as a record with attributes. You can tag, you can filter, you can automate on a signup or a date. What is harder is asking a question whose answer depends on a sequence of behaviours over time.

When is Mailchimp the better fit?

When the sending pattern is broadcasts, newsletters and a welcome sequence.

Paying for behavioural segmentation you will not use is a common and avoidable cost.

A well-run welcome sequence performs the same in either platform.

Count what you will actually send. A business running a newsletter, a seasonal promotion and a welcome sequence is paying for behavioural infrastructure it will never configure, and the money is better spent on the design and the list.

There is also a maintenance cost to the more capable platform. Behavioural flows need someone to own them; abandoned flows that fire stale offers do real damage to a sender reputation.

Does the platform affect design?

Less than people expect. Deliverable, mobile-first design is a discipline, not a feature.

Both platforms will happily send a template that breaks in Outlook. Both will send a clean one.

Our email work is built mobile-first and tested across clients regardless of which platform sends it.

Mobile-first is the discipline that actually decides performance, and it is platform-independent. A single-column layout, tappable targets, a subject line that survives truncation on a phone lock screen, and alt text that carries the message when images are blocked all matter more than which platform pressed send.

How do you decide?

Write down the segments you actually want to send to. The answer falls out of that list.

If the list reads like product names and purchase dates, you need Klaviyo. If it reads like signup sources and interests, you do not.

Our email marketing service starts with that list rather than with a platform recommendation.

Write the list of segments as plain English sentences before opening either platform. Then mark which ones need purchase or browse history to answer. If that column is empty, you have your answer and you have saved a monthly fee.

Do the same for the flows. 'Welcome', 'win-back' and 'post-purchase' are platform-agnostic names; 'replenishment timed to the product's consumption cycle' is not.

What about deliverability?

Deliverability follows your sending behaviour, not your platform.

Authentication on your own sending domain, a list built from genuine opt-ins, consistent volume and prompt suppression of bounces and complaints do the work. Both platforms support all of that; neither can compensate for a purchased list.

The fastest way to damage a domain is a large send to an old list after months of silence. Warm back up gradually, start with the segment that engaged most recently, and let the reputation rebuild before widening.

Can you run both?

You can, and some businesses do, but it is usually a sign of an unresolved decision.

Split sending means split suppression lists, which is how a customer who unsubscribed from one platform receives a campaign from the other. That is a compliance problem, not a tidiness problem.

If there is a genuine reason, for example transactional email in one system and marketing in the other, make the suppression sync explicit and test it rather than assuming it.
