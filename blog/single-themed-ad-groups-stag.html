Single Themed Ad Groups: Why STAG Still Works

By Digital Picto October 21, 2026 9 min read

A Single Themed Ad Group, or STAG, contains keywords that all express the same intent, so one ad and one landing page can answer every search that triggers them. We use the structure across Google Ads accounts because it keeps relevance high, and relevance is what Google rewards with quality score, impression share and lower cost per click.

What is a Single Themed Ad Group?

An ad group where every keyword expresses the same underlying intent, so a single ad can genuinely answer all of them.

The alternative, grouping loosely related keywords together, forces the ad copy to be generic enough to cover all of them. Generic copy means lower click-through, lower quality score and higher cost.

STAG is not about having one keyword per ad group. It is about having one intent per ad group. Several close variants of the same search belong together; two different questions do not.

The argument is mechanical. An ad group is the unit at which ad copy is written, so it is also the unit at which copy can be specific. One theme per group means every ad can answer one query exactly.

Broad groups force the copy to cover everything the group contains, which means covering nothing precisely, which is what below-average ad relevance looks like in the interface.

What results does the structure produce?

Across three published campaigns using STAG, click-through rates ranged from 2.50% to 8.72% with cost per click between $0.36 and $2.33.

For an eLearning platform, STAG with intent-matched keywords produced a 5.99% click-through rate at $0.36 per click.

For Builderall, the same structure across a four-month campaign returned 2.50% CTR at $0.47 and over 1,000 signups.

In US health insurance, one of the most competitive categories on the platform, STAG combined with a heavily optimised landing page reached 8.72% CTR and a 9.90% conversion rate.

The practical build is to group by intent rather than by keyword similarity. 'Emergency plumber' and '24 hour plumber' are one theme; 'plumber near me' and 'bathroom installation' are not, however similar the account structure makes them look.

Cross-negative between groups so each serves only its own theme. Without that, the structure exists on paper and collapses in delivery.

How does ad group structure affect cost?

Relevance raises quality score, and quality score lowers what you pay for the same position.

Google is deciding, on every auction, whether your ad is a good answer to the search. A tightly themed ad group makes that judgement easy in your favour.

The compounding effect matters: better relevance means more impressions at a lower cost, which produces more data, which makes optimisation faster.

The cost is maintenance, and it is real. More groups mean more ads, more negatives and more reporting rows, which is why the structure is usually abandoned by accounts that built it without deciding who maintains it.

The payoff is concentrated in competitive categories, where the relevance advantage translates directly into impression share you could not otherwise afford.

Does STAG still make sense with automated bidding?

Yes. Automation sets the bid; structure still determines what the bid is competing for and what the searcher sees.

Smart bidding has reduced the value of manual bid management, not the value of relevance. The ad and landing page a searcher reaches are still decided by your structure.

In the Builderall campaign we deliberately adapted the structure ahead of a Google Ads update so performance held through the change, which is only possible when the structure is deliberate in the first place.

It also makes the account legible. When each group has one theme, the search terms report tells you which intents convert, and the budget decisions become obvious rather than inferred.

That legibility is what we use it for as much as the performance: in the health insurance campaign and the process server campaign it is what made the conversion rates attributable to specific intents.

How do you decide what counts as one theme?

If one sentence of ad copy can honestly answer every keyword in the group, it is one theme.

Apply that test literally. Write the headline first, then check each keyword against it. Any keyword the headline does not answer belongs in a different ad group.

Match types do the rest. We combine broad, phrase and exact match deliberately to control how closely keywords match the search terms that trigger the ads.

How many keywords per ad group?

As few as express one intent, often a handful.

The number is an output, not a target. If adding a keyword forces the ad copy to become more general, it belongs in a different group.

Variants of the same intent, including close misspellings and word order, belong together. Different intents never do, regardless of volume.

Does this still matter with broad match and smart bidding?

Yes, for a different reason than it used to.

Automation decides who sees the ad; it does not write the ad. The relevance of the copy to the query remains yours to control, and it still feeds Quality Score.

If anything the structure matters more now, because broad matching widens the range of queries each group must answer, and a tight theme is what keeps the answer specific.
