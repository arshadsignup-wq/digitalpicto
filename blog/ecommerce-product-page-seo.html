Product Page SEO When Every Page Looks the Same

By Digital Picto November 6, 2026 9 min read

Large catalogues commonly fail in search because templated product pages differ only by a name and a price, which reads as near-duplication. Google crawls them and declines to index. The fix is giving each page something genuinely its own, and it is usually the only thing standing between the catalogue and traffic.

Why do catalogues go unindexed?

Because hundreds of pages sharing a template and differing by a few words look like the same page repeated.

Manufacturer descriptions make this worse, since the identical text appears on every retailer's site.

On a grocery delivery site we worked on, a large share of the catalogue was being held out of the index entirely. Fixing that came before any keyword work.

Manufacturer copy is the single biggest structural problem in ecommerce SEO, because it makes your page identical to every other retailer selling the same item. There is no reason for a search engine to prefer yours, and usually a reason to prefer the brand's own.

The fix is not rewriting for the sake of difference. It is adding the information the manufacturer has no reason to supply: how it fits, what it compares to, what people ask before buying, and what arrives in the box.

What makes a product page unique enough?

Content that answers what a buyer actually asks before purchasing.

Product FAQs are the highest-leverage version of this: real questions, answered on the page, different for every product.

On that same site the groundwork was unglamorous: clearing 4 errors, 402 warnings and 66 notices before any content work, then writing the page copy the catalogue had never had.

Variants are where product SEO quietly breaks. Twenty colourways on twenty URLs with the same copy produces twenty thin pages competing with each other, and canonical tags that point in inconsistent directions make it worse.

Decide deliberately: one canonical product page with variant selection, or separate pages where the variant has genuine independent demand. Size rarely does. Colour sometimes does. Model and capacity usually do.

Does schema help?

It helps Google understand the page and can earn rich results, but it does not rescue thin content.

Product schema with price, availability and reviews is worth implementing. It is a multiplier on a good page, not a substitute for one.

Our SEO work treats schema as the last step after indexation and content, not the first.

Product schema earns its place when it is complete and accurate: price, currency, availability, condition, and review data that actually exists on the page. Partial markup gets ignored; inaccurate markup gets penalised.

Keep availability in sync. Markup claiming in-stock on a sold-out product is the most common cause of an otherwise healthy feed being distrusted.

How do you prioritise a large catalogue?

Start with the products that already get traffic or margin, not alphabetically.

A catalogue of 200 products does not need 200 rewrites at once. The top sellers carry most of the opportunity.

Fix those, confirm they index and rank, then work down.

Reviews and questions are the only content on a product page that scales without a writer, and they answer the long-tail queries your copy never will. Surfacing them as crawlable text rather than inside a script is the whole job.

The second benefit is behavioural. A page that answers the pre-purchase question keeps the visitor on it, and the alternative is a return to the results page that search engines can read.

What about out-of-stock and discontinued products?

Keep the page, change what it does.

Deleting a product page discards the links and the ranking it accumulated, and returns a 404 to people who are actively looking for that item. Keeping it and offering the replacement captures that demand.

For a permanently discontinued item, redirect to the closest successor rather than the category page. A redirect to a category reads as a dead end to both the visitor and the crawler.

How do product pages fit the wider site?

They convert; category and guide pages win the broader queries that feed them.

A product page ranks for the product. The query that precedes it, the comparison, the fit question, the best-for query, belongs on a page built for it, which then links down to the products.

That structure also gives product pages internal links from somewhere other than the navigation, which is usually the difference between indexed and ignored for a large catalogue.
